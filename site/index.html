xscapes

A thinking screen for terminal agents. Claude Code runs inside a shoreline: the sea rises while it works, the sand records what it did, and a cat knocks when it needs you.

One Go binary, no dependencies. Terminal.app, iTerm2, anything 256-colour. MIT. github.com/donlucasx/xscapes

go install github.com/donlucasx/xscapes@latest
xscapes install claude --apply  # writes the hooks after a backup; uninstall restores byte for byte

Why

You send a prompt and the terminal goes quiet. You tab away and forget to come back, or you sit and watch a spinner that says nothing. Agents now think for minutes at a time, and that time has no face.

xscapes gives it one. A glance at the window tells you how hard the agent is working, what it is doing right now, whether something broke, and whether it is waiting on you. Nothing to read, nothing to configure, and the agent's own screen is untouched.

What you are looking at

Everything on screen means one thing, and no two things share a channel. The water is the work. The sky is the world.

how hard the agent is workingthe sea: how many swells are travelling, how tall, whitecaps above half
what it is doing right nowwriting in the sand: newest brightest, older lines taken by the tide
something is brokenthe companion: ears back, tail flat, amber eyes, and it stays that way until you come back
it needs youa solid balloon in a warm colour, plus a bright chime
it finisheda dotted balloon in a cool colour, plus a low sonar note
subagentskittens, one per agent, some of them swimming
todos completedstars in the upper sky, one lit per finished item
context leftthe sun by day, the moon by night: one body, phase and height
time of daythe sky, from your actual clock

That rule is why a glance works. Sea state always means the agent. Sky, light and time always mean reality. You never have to ask which of two meanings a change is carrying.

Two knocks

"I finished" and "I am blocked on you" are different messages and never look the same. The two balloons differ in shape as well as colour, so they survive a screenshot and a colourblind reading, and they carry different sounds, so they survive you looking at another window. Silent while the agent works.

The waiting layer

xscapes is two things. Underneath is an event protocol with pluggable adapters, which is the part that works for any agent. On top is a reference scape, which is what that protocol looks like when you give it a sea and a cat.

session_start  prompt  tool_start  tool_end  error  test_pass  test_fail
sub_start  sub_end  needs_input  done  compact  context  todo  session_end

Adapters translate an agent into those events. The engine folds events into a scene. Tool events do not each get their own weather; every one feeds a single activity level, and the sand names the tool and the file. The sea says how much, the sand says what.

  • Claude Code, via hooks. One command installs them into your settings after a backup, merging with whatever hooks you already run. The payload schema was read out of the Claude Code binary rather than guessed.
  • Any process (specified, not yet built): alive plus output means busy, the prompt coming back means done.
  • Anything else: xscapes emit needs_input -text "allow Bash?" from a shell script is a complete adapter.

Native to the terminal

It runs where the agent runs. No browser tab, no second window, nothing to alt-tab to. The agent gets the top of the window and the shoreline reads downward from it: sky, sea, beach. A taller window spends its extra rows on beach, where the work is written.

xscapes is deliberately not a terminal emulator. The agent runs on a pty sized to its own band, held there by a scroll region, and its bytes reach your terminal untouched. Nothing xscapes fails to understand can corrupt the agent's display. The colours are chosen from what a 256-colour terminal actually holds, so the picture above is the picture you get.

Status

Early and running daily. The scape, the Claude Code adapter, both sounds and the launcher work. The companion's final coat is still being chosen. A 60-second screen recording of a real turn is next.
